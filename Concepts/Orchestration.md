Orchestration is the craft of deciding which instruments play which notes in a piece of music, and how those instruments are combined — it's the difference between writing notes on a page and deciding "this melody should be a solo oboe over softly held strings" versus "this melody should be doubled by three instruments at once for a bigger sound." Composers have practiced orchestration for centuries mostly by ear and tradition, but researchers in music cognition have started asking a more scientific question: what actually happens in a listener's perception when instruments are combined in different ways? Do certain combinations make a melody stand out, or cause it to blend into the background, or create the impression of one instrument's timbre "coloring" another? Studying orchestration perceptually connects the art of writing for orchestra to concepts like timbre and auditory scene analysis, turning practical composer intuition into testable psychological questions.
