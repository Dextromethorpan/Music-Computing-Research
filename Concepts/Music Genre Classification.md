Music genre classification is the task of teaching a computer to label a piece of music with a category like "rock," "jazz," "classical," or "hip-hop," based only on its audio. It is one of the oldest and most influential problems in music information retrieval (MIR): early systems extracted numerical descriptions of timbre, rhythm, and pitch from short audio clips and fed them into machine-learning classifiers trained on labeled examples. The field's most famous benchmark, the GTZAN dataset (1,000 thirty-second clips across ten genres), was built specifically to test these systems and became the default yardstick researchers used for two decades — even though later studies found flaws in it, such as duplicate tracks and mislabeled songs, which is now an important cautionary lesson about relying too heavily on one dataset. Genre classification research helped establish the basic MIR pipeline (extract audio features, train a classifier, evaluate accuracy) that later spread to related tasks like mood detection and instrument recognition.
