Congenital amusia — often called "tone deafness" in everyday language — is a lifelong difficulty perceiving or producing music that isn't caused by hearing loss, brain damage, or lack of exposure to music. People with the condition usually have completely normal speech and hearing, but struggle to tell whether a melody is going up or down in pitch, notice when a note is out of tune, or sing back a simple tune. Researchers believe it stems from atypical wiring between the parts of the brain that process pitch and the parts that connect that information to memory and motor control (like the voice). It's studied both because it reveals which parts of music processing are innate versus learned, and because it runs in families, suggesting a genetic component. Diagnosing it typically uses standardized tests such as the Montreal Battery of Evaluation of Amusia (MBEA), which checks skills like pitch discrimination and rhythm perception against a normal-population baseline.
