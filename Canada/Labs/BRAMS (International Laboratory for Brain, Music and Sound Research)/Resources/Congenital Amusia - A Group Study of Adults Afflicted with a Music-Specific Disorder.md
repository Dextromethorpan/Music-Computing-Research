This paper is one of the founding studies establishing "congenital amusia" (commonly called tone deafness) as a real, measurable condition rather than just poor musical taste or lack of practice. The researchers tested 11 adults who reported lifelong difficulty with music, using a battery of tasks originally designed to diagnose music problems in brain-damaged patients. They found these adults had normal hearing and normal speech processing, but consistently failed at telling whether melodies changed pitch correctly, at remembering tunes, at singing back a melody, and at tapping along with a beat. The core deficit centers on perceiving small pitch changes — the participants could hear that a sound was present, but not reliably tell whether it moved up or down. This showed that musical ability can fail selectively while every other form of hearing and cognition stays intact, meaning the brain likely has partly separate machinery just for music.
