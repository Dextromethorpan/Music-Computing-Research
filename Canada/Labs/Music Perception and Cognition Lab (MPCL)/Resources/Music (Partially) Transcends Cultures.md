This Frontiers in Psychology study asked a deceptively simple question: does music trigger the same bodily and emotional reactions in people from very different cultures? The researchers played a variety of musical excerpts to Congolese Pygmy participants (a hunter-gatherer community with little exposure to Western music) and to Canadian participants, measuring both physiological responses (like changes in heart rate and skin conductance, which reflect arousal) and self-reported feelings. The results showed that physiological arousal responses were fairly similar across the two groups — bodies reacted in comparable ways regardless of cultural background — but the specific emotions people said they felt (like calling something "happy" or "sad") were more shaped by cultural familiarity. The study attracted wide media attention because it offered evidence for a middle-ground answer to the old "is music a universal language" debate: partly yes, partly no, depending on what aspect of the response you look at.
