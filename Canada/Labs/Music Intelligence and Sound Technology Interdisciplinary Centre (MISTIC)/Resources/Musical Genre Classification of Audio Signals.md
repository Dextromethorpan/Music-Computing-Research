This is the single most-cited paper to come out of Tzanetakis's early research, published in IEEE Transactions on Speech and Audio Processing and later awarded the IEEE Signal Processing Society's 2004 Young Author Best Paper Award. It tackles a simple-sounding question: can a computer listen to a 30-second clip of music and correctly guess its genre (rock, jazz, classical, and so on) without any human-written rules, just by learning statistical patterns from labeled examples? The paper introduces a now-standard set of audio "features" — numerical summaries of timbre (tone color), rhythm, and pitch content extracted directly from the sound wave — that get fed into a machine-learning classifier. To test the approach, the authors built and released the GTZAN dataset (1,000 labeled 30-second clips across 10 genres), which became the field's default benchmark for two decades. This paper essentially defined the standard MIR pipeline still taught today: extract features, train a classifier, measure accuracy.
