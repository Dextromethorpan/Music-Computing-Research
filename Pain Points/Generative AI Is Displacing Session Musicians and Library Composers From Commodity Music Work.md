A large share of working musicians' income doesn't come from headline releases — it comes from "functional" commodity work: sync placements, stock beats, production-library cues, and ad soundtracks. Tools like Suno, Udio, and AIVA can now generate a complete, radio-ready track in this style in under two minutes at near-zero cost, which puts direct downward pressure specifically on the session musicians, songwriters, and library composers who relied on this steady, craft-based (not celebrity-based) income, rather than on star artists.

## Evidence
A 2024 study commissioned by CISAC (the international confederation of authors' and composers' societies) projected that generative AI could cost human musicians up to 24% of revenue by 2028 — roughly $10 billion annually across the global music industry — with streaming royalties, licensing fees, and session work specifically named as at risk as AI-generated tracks fill playlists, background-music libraries, and advertising slots. Industry reporting on the resulting displacement identifies session musicians, smaller studios, and library composers doing commodity work as the group most exposed, since exactly the kind of functional, formulaic music they produce is what generation tools reproduce most convincingly. The scale of concern within the professional music community is itself evidence this isn't a marginal complaint: the artist protests of 2025 saw Kate Bush, Paul McCartney, and over 1,000 other musicians release a silent protest album specifically over AI's encroachment on their craft and livelihood.
