Researchers and interaction designers regularly build genuinely novel digital musical instruments — new interfaces, sensor-based controllers, embodied interaction systems — and present them at a conference or in a paper. But once that debut is over, most of these instruments simply stop being used, including by the very people who built them. This is a recognized, named problem in the field itself: instruments exist as one-off demos rather than things musicians can actually keep learning, performing on, or acquiring.

## Evidence
A dedicated study, "Design for Longevity: Ongoing Use of Instruments from NIME 2010–14," contacted 97 digital musical instrument (DMI) makers who had presented new instruments across five consecutive years of the NIME conference and confirmed that most of the instruments failed to establish any ongoing use after their debut. The paper's own framing describes this as "a prevalent feeling" within the NIME research community itself, not a fringe observation — strong enough that it has generated a small cluster of dedicated follow-up research specifically on the problem, including "Ephemeral Instruments," "The Problem of DMI Adoption and Longevity: Envisioning a NIME Performance Pedagogy," and "DMI Apprenticeship: Sharing and Replicating Musical Artefacts." Identified causes include lack of ongoing technical support, thin documentation, and incompatibility with newer hardware/software as the original creator moves on.
