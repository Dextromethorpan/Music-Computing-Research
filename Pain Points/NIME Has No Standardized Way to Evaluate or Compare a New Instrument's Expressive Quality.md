Unlike, say, benchmark accuracy in machine learning, there's no agreed-upon way to measure whether a newly-built digital musical instrument is actually "good" — expressive, nuanced, suited to its intended musical context. Most NIME papers that do attempt formal evaluation rely on ad-hoc questionnaires the designer wrote themselves, which vary from paper to paper and often don't meet real scientific rigor. That makes it genuinely hard to compare two different instruments' expressive quality, to build cumulative knowledge across the field about what design choices actually improve expressiveness, or to know whether a positive evaluation reflects the instrument's real qualities versus the specific (often self-authored) questionnaire used to measure it.

## Evidence
Research surveying the field's own evaluation practices finds that the number of NIME papers employing formal evaluation methodology has stayed low, and that there are only "limited options in terms of formal standardized tools specifically developed for DMI evaluation," with "many evaluations making use of ad-hoc questionnaires developed by designers or researchers themselves that may not pass scientific rigor." The field's own direct response confirms the gap is recognized and being actively worked on: Schmid's MPX-Q (Musician's Perception of the Experiential Quality of Musical Instruments Questionnaire) was built specifically on psychometric principles to be a reliable, valid, reusable instrument-evaluation tool, and separate crowd-sourced tagging approaches — where many people assign descriptive keywords to an instrument, later cluster-analyzed — represent a distinct methodological attempt to get standardized signal out of an inherently subjective judgment.
