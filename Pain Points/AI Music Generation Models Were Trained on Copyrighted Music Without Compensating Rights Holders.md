Neural network-based music generation models learn by training on enormous amounts of existing recorded music. Several major generative-music companies trained their models on copyrighted commercial recordings without a license or the rights holders' permission, and are now generating and monetizing new music that competes directly with the artists whose work trained the system in the first place. This is a real, ongoing financial harm to labels and artists, not a hypothetical one — it's currently the subject of major litigation.

## Evidence
In June 2024 the RIAA filed lawsuits against both Suno and Udio, initially naming roughly 560 works Suno had allegedly trained on without authorization. Discovery escalated significantly in late 2025: an expert retained by Universal and Sony physically inspected Suno's training dataset at its law firm and matched it against an audio-fingerprint system, leading plaintiffs to move in May 2026 to expand the complaint to 61,026 works. The cases have started resolving unevenly rather than being fully vindicated for artists — Universal reached a settlement with Udio in October 2025 (a royalty of roughly $0.002–$0.005 per generation plus a joint licensed platform) and Warner Music separately licensed with Suno in November 2025, while Sony's claims against Udio and Universal/Sony's case against Suno remain unresolved, with Suno still fighting on fair-use grounds toward a July 2026 summary judgment hearing.
