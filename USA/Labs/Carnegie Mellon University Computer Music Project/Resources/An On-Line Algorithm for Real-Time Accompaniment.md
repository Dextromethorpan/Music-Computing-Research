This 1984 paper, presented at the International Computer Music Conference, is one of the founding works in "score following" — software that listens to a live musician playing from written music and automatically tracks which note they're currently playing, so a computer accompaniment can stay in sync with the human performer even if they speed up, slow down, or make a small mistake. Dannenberg's algorithm compares the live performance to the written score using a matching technique (dynamic programming, a general method for finding the best alignment between two sequences) and introduces "virtual time," a flexible time reference that speeds up or slows down along with the soloist. This work laid the foundation for decades of later research on computer-accompanied performance.
