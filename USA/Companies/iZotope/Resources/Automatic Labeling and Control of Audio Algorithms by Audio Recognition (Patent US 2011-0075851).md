This early iZotope patent (published 2011, later granted as US 9,031,243) describes a system that listens to an audio signal and automatically figures out what's in it — for example, recognizing that a sound is "male speech," "applause," or "electric guitar" — then uses that recognition to automatically configure how the software processes it. The process happens in stages: first basic low-level signal analysis (measuring raw acoustic properties), then statistical and perceptual processing (comparing those measurements to what humans perceive as meaningful categories), and finally a machine-learning stage that assigns symbolic labels or "tags" to the sound. Those labels can then drive audio software, instruments, or recording gear to automatically pick the right processing chain for the content, instead of requiring a person to manually choose settings. This idea — using automatic content recognition to drive smart, content-aware audio processing — underlies many of iZotope's later "assistive audio" features across products like RX, Neutron, and Ozone.
