A filter is a circuit that lets some pitches (frequencies) of a sound through while blocking others — like a tone knob, but much more powerful. In 1966 Bob Moog filed this patent for a "voltage-controlled" filter: instead of turning a knob by hand, you feed it an electrical voltage and the filter's cutoff point moves automatically, which is what lets a synthesizer "sweep" a sound and make it feel alive. His trick was to build the filter out of a chain, or "ladder," of paired transistors, using a quirk of how transistors naturally resist electricity to shape the sound instead of needing bulkier parts. This became known as the Moog ladder filter, and it is the exact circuit design still used inside modern Moog synthesizers today, more than fifty years later — a rare case of a single patented circuit still defining a whole product line and a large slice of what "analog synth sound" means.
