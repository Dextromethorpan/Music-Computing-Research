This page explains how Arturia recreates instruments that have real moving, vibrating parts — pianos, electric pianos like the Fender Rhodes, organs, brass instruments — using "physical modeling" instead of recording samples of them. Physical modeling means building a mathematical simulation of the actual physics of an instrument (how a string vibrates, how a hammer strikes it, how the material of its body resonates) rather than just replaying short audio recordings of notes. The page walks through why sample-based instruments feel "static": they can only capture a limited number of loudness levels and struggle to reproduce the way a real instrument's sound continuously and subtly changes as you play it. It also states that Arturia's Brass instrument (2006) was developed in partnership with IRCAM, the French national institute for music and acoustic research, using IRCAM's own physical modeling research, and credits Stanford University alongside IRCAM as the two places where the foundational science of physical modeling was developed starting in the 1970s. This is genuine evidence of Arturia collaborating with an established academic acoustics research institute, not just an in-house marketing claim.
