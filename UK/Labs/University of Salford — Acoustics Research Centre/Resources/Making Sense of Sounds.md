Humans are remarkably good at instantly recognising what's making a sound — a dog bark, a door slam, footsteps — but teaching a computer to do the same (called "machine listening") is hard. This EPSRC-funded project, led on the perceptual side by Salford's Trevor Cox and Bill Davies in partnership with the University of Surrey's signal processing group, set out to bridge that gap. The team built a large dataset of everyday sound recordings, developed a new scientific "taxonomy" (classification scheme) of how people naturally group and describe everyday sounds, and ran an international machine-learning competition challenging researchers to build automatic sound-classification systems and compare them against genuine human perception. While much of the sound library covers non-musical everyday sounds, the underlying methods — matching machine listening systems to how humans actually group and label sound — connect directly to how music-related audio-recognition tools are built and evaluated.
